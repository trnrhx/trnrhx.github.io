Mental Checklist 🧭

The 4-Step Positional Decision Loop

Before touching a piece in the middlegame or endgame, ask yourself in strict order:

  1. Prophylaxis First: What is my opponent's immediate threat or next active idea? Can I strangle it before it begins?
  2. Endgame Transition Check: If all major pieces were traded right now, who wins the King-and-Pawn endgame?
  3. Worst-Placed Piece: Which of my pieces has the least scope, and what is its ideal outpost or open file?
  4. Color Complex & Pawns: Are my pawns placed on the opposite color of my remaining Bishop?
Source: Chess Fundamentals, Part I
Simplification Logic ⚖️

When to Trade Pieces (and When to Refuse)

Beginners trade pieces randomly because tension feels uncomfortable. Capablanca traded only with concrete purpose:

  • ✓ Trade Major Pieces when you are ahead in material or when your opponent has a fractured pawn structure (more pawn islands) that is easier to attack without Queens on the board.
  • ✓ Eliminate the Defender: Trade off your opponent's only active piece or the piece guarding weak squares.
  • ✗ Never Trade Pawns when you are behind in material! Wait to trade pieces when behind, and trade pawns when fighting for a draw.
Principle: Petit Combinations & Simplification
Endgame Axioms ♜

Rook & King Endgame Rules of Thumb

Capablanca was the greatest Rook endgame player in history. Memorize these four iron laws:

  • 1. Initiative Over Material: In Rook endings, an active Rook on the 7th rank + an active central King is worth 1 to 2 pawns more than a passive Rook defending pawns.
  • 2. Cut Off the Enemy King: Use your Rook along a file or rank to build an invisible barrier preventing the opposing King from approaching the action.
  • 3. Rooks Belong Behind Passed Pawns: Whether yours or your opponent's—their scope increases as the pawn advances.
  • 4. Advance King & Pawn Together: Never push pawns prematurely without your King in support.
Illustrated in: Capablanca–Tartakower, NY 1924
Minor Piece Mastery ♞

Capablanca's Theorem of Bishop & Pawns

Capablanca formulated one of the clearest positional rules in chess literature regarding minor pieces:

When YOU have a Bishop: Keep your pawns on squares of the opposite color of your Bishop so the diagonals remain open and unobstructed.

When YOUR OPPONENT has a Bishop: Force or fix your pawns (and theirs!) onto squares of the same color as their Bishop to turn it into a "tall pawn," while anchoring your Knight on an unassailable outpost of the opposite color.

Source: Chess Fundamentals, Section 11
Pawn Islands ⛓️

Pawn Structure & Majority Creation

Every disconnected group of pawns is a "pawn island" whose base requires piece protection.

  • • Fewer Islands = Healthier Structure: A compact 2-island formation (e.g., a-b-c and f-g-h) is superior to 3 or 4 scattered islands.
  • • Mobile Queenside Majority: In Capablanca vs. Marshall (1909), Marshall had a 4-vs-3 queenside pawn majority but failed to advance it. Capablanca demonstrated that a healthy pawn majority must be mobilized to create a passed pawn while restraining the opponent's majority.

Calculation Discipline 🔍

"La Petite Combinaison" (The Little Combination)

Capablanca rarely needed wild 12-move sacrifices because he chained together short, concrete 2-to-3 move tactical sequences ("petite combinations") that transformed one small advantage into another:

Instead of hunting for checkmate from move 15, look for a 3-move sequence that trades off your bad Bishop for their good Knight, wins control of the single open c-file, or forces a permanent backward pawn on d6.
